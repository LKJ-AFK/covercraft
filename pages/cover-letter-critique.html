Cover Letter Critique: 7 Mistakes That Get Your Letter Ignored

You sent dozens of applications and heard nothing back. The letter is usually the reason. 1. The template opener

Bad: "I am writing to express my keen interest in the Software Engineer position at your company."

Why it kills your application: Recruiters see this exact opener hundreds of times a day. It says nothing about you, the role, or the company — it signals a mass-applied letter and gets skimmed past in seconds.

Fix: Open with a 2-sentence hook: name the exact role and one concrete reason you are applying (a product feature, a blog post, a mission point), plus one line of proof of your strongest match.

2. Banned clichés — "passionate", "excited", "eager"

Bad: "I am a passionate software developer with a strong background in building web applications."

Why it kills your application: Self-praise with no evidence. Every applicant claims passion; the adjective adds nothing the recruiter can verify, and it is the single most common tell of an AI-generated or template letter.

Fix: Cut the adjective and show the work instead: name one project, one tool, and one result. "I rebuilt our dashboard pipeline in SQL and cut reporting time from 3 days to 4 hours" beats "passionate developer" every time.

3. Empty claims — "perfect fit", "significant contribution"

Bad: "I believe my skills and experience make me a perfect fit for this role."

Why it kills your application: It is an assertion with no proof, and it could appear in any application to any company. Recruiters mentally delete sentences like this.

Fix: Replace it with the closest matching evidence: a bullet that mirrors a requirement in the job ad — the tool, the scale, the outcome.

4. An anonymized employer — "a technology company"

Bad: "In my previous role at a technology company, I was responsible for developing and maintaining various software projects."

Why it kills your application: Hiding the employer reads as if you are ashamed of it or have something to hide. Vague duties ("various software projects") compound the vagueness.

Fix: Name the employer and one specific system you worked on. If confidentiality is a real concern, name the industry and product type — "at a logistics startup, the delivery-tracking platform" — and quantify the scope.

5. Generic teamwork — "cross-functional teams"

Bad: "I worked closely with cross-functional teams to deliver high-quality products."

Why it kills your application: This sentence could describe any employee at any company. Without specifics, it adds zero information.

Fix: Say what the team shipped and your role in it: "I owned the analytics module in a 6-person squad that launched a mobile app to 40,000 users."

6. Zero numbers — the kiss of death

Bad: "I have extensive experience with JavaScript, Python, and cloud technologies."

Why it kills your application: A skills list with no scale, no outcome, and no responsibility attached tells the recruiter nothing about impact. In a stack of similar applicants, the one with numbers wins.

Fix: Attach a metric to every skill: users served, latency cut, revenue moved, uptime kept. If you do not have a number, use the size of the system or the team.

7. The passive closing — "I look forward to hearing from you"

Bad: "Thank you for considering my application. I look forward to hearing from you soon."

Why it kills your application: It is boilerplate with no call to action. The letter ends exactly like hundreds of others, so nothing makes the recruiter want to reply.

Fix: Close with a confident, specific call to action: offer to walk through a relevant project and state your availability — name the thing you will show them, not a generic thanks.
